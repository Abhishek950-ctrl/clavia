The review pane, grown into
a working add-in

The third pane on a contract — Playbook, Ask Clavia — is where Clavia stands in for a Word add-in. We took every gap from the competitor research and built it into that pane, on top of what was there: the same cards, sheets, menus and toasts. Then we added what nobody has. Everything below runs on Acme’s redline of our MSA (REQ-149).

Parity. Run setup, progress, the playbook’s clause one click away, benchmarks against our own deals, their comments answered, sign-off, skills, defined terms, an issues list and a note for the requester.
Ahead. What their changes add up to, countered as a package; counterparty history turned into a one-turn response; per-rule confidence; overrides that become playbook proposals; approvals that go stale.

The five, and where they show up

  1. Trade-offs across clauses. The exposure line and Counter as a package (feature 3).
  2. Counterparty memory. Acme’s history and Close in one turn (feature 4).
  3. A tested playbook. Per-rule confidence on our own contracts (feature 8). The playbook’s own test tab is next.
  4. A playbook that learns, with an owner. Make this the playbook’s answer → proposal → Add to playbook as a new version (features 12–13).
  5. Law changes that ripple. A rule changed by a Horizon item says so on the card, with a link to it (Termination, HZ-231).
What we built

Feature by feature

01

Run setup

One line under the playbook name says how the review was run: the published version, whose paper, our side, and Strict or Deal-closing. Click it to change any of them, stack up to two more playbooks (MSA + DPA), and re-run.

Who else
Harvey has Strict/Permissive; Ivo asks deal-context questions and stacks up to 3 playbooks. Microsoft and Legora have neither.
Why ours is better
Prefilled from the request (deal value, region, counterparty), so nobody fills in a form. Deal-closing changes which wording each card opens on: inside a fallback it offers “Accept their change”.
02

Progress and finishing

“3 of 14 reviewed” sits beside the playbook name. The ⋯ menu finishes the job: apply every suggestion at once, mark all reviewed, download the issues list as a Word table, write a summary for the requester, save as v4.

Who else
Harvey shows reviewed/unreviewed; GC AI and Ivo export issues lists and memos.
Why ours is better
Reviewed means applied, accepted, dismissed, signed off or replied to — no extra checkbox to tick.
03

The deal as a whole

Above the clauses, one line says what their changes add up to: “Together, their changes leave us uncapped for a data breach.” Counter as a package shows the three moves (§9, §7, §10) and applies them as one counter, with Undo.

Who else
Nobody. Every product reviewed checks one clause at a time.
Why ours is better
This is the first of our five: trade-offs across clauses. A lawyer sees the exposure before reading twelve verdicts.
04

Counterparty memory

A second line knows Acme: usually 3 turns, always pushes on the cap and payment. Close in one turn accepts the 6 changes that sit inside our playbook and counters the 4 outside it with our fallbacks — “Expected: 1 turn instead of 3.”

Who else
Luminance remembers the counterparty; Ivo estimates extra turns per position. Neither turns it into the response.
Why ours is better
Second of our five, inside the review rather than a dashboard.
05

A fuller clause card

Each clause now carries why we hold the position, what our own signed deals agreed (“12 months in 8, 24 months in 4… never uncapped”), and their comment from the Word file, answered in place. The redundant “Their position” card is gone.

Who else
Harvey and Ivo benchmark against executed deals; GC AI stores rationale; Ivo and Claude for Word read comment bubbles.
Why ours is better
All three on one card, in a few quiet lines, each one click from its source.
06

The playbook’s own clause

“View in playbook” opens the clause as the playbook defines it, in the pane’s bottom sheet: why it holds, the starting position, what is not acceptable and each fallback, with the redline that gets the contract there and Apply.

Who else
Harvey cites the rule each clause matched.
Why ours is better
You asked for this: the playbook was invisible from the review. Now every clause is one click from its rule, and its version (“v4 · Published”).
07

Our deals behind a number

See deals lists the twelve signed MSAs over £1M behind the benchmark line, with what each agreed for this clause.

Who else
Ivo shows a percentile; Harvey a precedent chart.
Why ours is better
Plain list a lawyer can quote from in the next email.
08

How sure Clavia is

Where Clavia has matched our lawyers less often on past contracts, the card says so: “Check this one: Clavia matched your lawyers on 19 of 28 past contracts for this rule.” Strong rules say nothing.

Who else
Nobody shows accuracy per rule on the customer’s own contracts. Ironclad validates once, before go-live.
Why ours is better
Third of our five, as it appears to the reviewer. The playbook-side test bench is next.
09

The card’s foot

Edit with AI and a quiet Not right? (wrong position, wrong clause, too aggressive, not aggressive enough) on the left; Ask Grace and Apply on the right. Ask appears only where the clause is outside the playbook.

Who else
Ivo has thumbs-down per markup; escalation is a text field almost everywhere.
Why ours is better
Feedback is logged against the rule for its owner, not lost.
10

Sign-off

Ask Grace opens a card: who decides (from the playbook’s escalation role), what they ask in one line, what we would offer, and Clavia’s view, with an optional note. Send puts it in Teams.

Who else
Ivo Collaborate (early access) has Settle / Counter / Escalate. Others route nothing.
Why ours is better
The owner sees the decision, not the document.
11

Approved, and stale when it changes

The row says “With Grace”, then “Grace approved”, with her note on the card. If the wording later changes, it says “Grace approved different wording · Ask again”. Replying to Acme’s comment offers Clavia’s reply as one chip; the box starts empty.

Who else
No one else marks an approval stale when the terms move.
Why ours is better
Already ours in Intake; now in the review where the wording changes.
12

Their change, accepted — and taught back

Where their wording sits inside the playbook, “Accept their change” is one of the wordings on offer. When you pick something other than Clavia’s pick, the card asks “Make this the playbook’s answer?”

Who else
GC AI’s Smart Check promotes one finding; Harvey’s update-from-deals is waitlisted.
Why ours is better
Fourth of our five: overrides become evidence, at the moment of the decision.
13

Proposals on the playbook

The playbook shows “1 change proposed from reviews”. Each card says what, from which review and counterparty, by whom; Add to playbook saves a new version (“New fallback on Fees and Payment”), Dismiss has Undo.

Who else
No competitor documents an approval step for changing a playbook.
Why ours is better
The loop closes: review → proposal → owner → new version → the next review.
14

Skills in Ask Clavia

Type / in Ask for skills: summarise their changes, check defined terms, pull out obligations and deadlines, draft the issues list.

Who else
Microsoft’s Copilot for Legal ships 20 skills; Legora and GC AI use “/” too.
Why ours is better
Each answers from this contract, each line pointing at its clause.
15

Defined terms, checked

Reads §1’s definitions and every cross-reference. On Acme’s MSA: Order Form, Subscription Term and Security Breach are used but never defined; all three cross-references point to the right clause.

Who else
Microsoft check-defined-terms; Definely.
Why ours is better
This one is real logic, not scripted: it reads the contract as it stands, redlines included.
16

Their changes, in plain words

What they changed, clause by clause, in one line each: “A 24-month cap, with unlimited liability for breaches of §7.”

Who else
Claude for Word summarises redlines by severity; Harvey shows every counterparty change.
Why ours is better
Written the way counsel would say it to a GC.
17

A note for the requester

Five plain lines for Grace from where the review stands: what they sent, what we countered, the one point that matters, what is with whom, what is left.

Who else
GC AI drafts emails; Ivo writes memos.
Why ours is better
Generated from the review’s real state; Copy, or open it in Outlook as a draft.
Honest notes

What is real, what is scripted, what is next

Real, in the prototype

  • Setup, re-run, stacking, Strict / Deal-closing changing each card’s default.
  • Progress, filters (Their changes, Not reviewed), Apply all, Mark all reviewed.
  • Accept their change, sign-off states including stale, replies saved as For-them comments.
  • Defined terms, cross-references, obligations and their-changes skills read the contract as it stands.
  • Issues list downloads as a Word table; the requester note is built from the review’s state.
  • Proposals reach the playbook; Add to playbook saves a real new version.

Scripted for the demo

  • Rationale, benchmarks, confidence figures, Acme’s history and comments, the exposure line (review-plus-data.js).
  • Grace’s approval arrives six seconds later; Teams and Outlook are toasts.
  • There is still no model behind the review; the five seeded reviews are precomputed.

Next

  • The playbook’s Test tab: the golden set behind the confidence figures.
  • Approvals across contracts, once the Approvals tab is shown again.
  • The same pane inside Word.

Design review

Two rounds on the pane. Fixed: two class names that collided with existing styles; type sizes snapped to the pane’s 12 / 13 / 14; deal lines aligned to the clause names (both at the same ink edge); native titles replaced with house tooltips; a duplicate reviewed tick beside “Grace approved”; sign-off offered only outside the playbook; setup no longer assumes our paper on an NDA on theirs. Removed at your request: the “Their position” card. Audit: no boxes inside boxes, no grey fills, nothing clipped, every icon button has a tooltip.
